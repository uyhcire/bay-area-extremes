Where a Bay Area income goes, in real dollars

The average Bay Area household takes in $184,755 a year, +79% against the U.S. average. Local prices run 117 against a national 100, and housing runs 197. Dividing each use of income by its own local price level leaves $150,956 of national-price purchasing power, +47% against the U.S. The percentage beside each branch is how much more of that thing, in real terms, a Bay Area household gets than the average American one.

How this was built

  • Income and its uses come from the BLS Consumer Expenditure Survey, 2023–24 two-year average, for the San Francisco–Oakland–Hayward metro (San Francisco, San Mateo, Alameda, Contra Costa and Marin counties; San Jose is a separate metro the survey does not publish) and for all U.S. consumer units. A consumer unit is roughly a household.
  • Real dollars divide each line by the matching BEA regional price parity for the San Francisco–Oakland–Fremont metro, averaged over the same two years: housing for shelter, utilities for utilities, goods for groceries, vehicles, fuel, clothing and furnishings, other services for the rest of consumption, and all items for taxes, saving and giving. U.S. lines are divided by the U.S. parities, which sit within about 1.5 points of 100.
  • Taxes are estimated, because BLS stopped publishing the survey's tax figures in 2024. Federal tax is survey income times the effective rate in IRS county data (total tax liability over adjusted gross income, tax year 2023): 20.1% for the metro's five counties, 14.8% nationally. State and local tax is federal tax times the ratio of state-and-local to federal personal taxes in BEA's accounts: 0.35 for California, 0.23 nationally. Property tax sits in shelter, and Social Security payroll tax sits with retirement contributions, where the survey puts them.
  • Net saving is what remains of income after taxes and every expenditure line, so it includes mortgage principal and absorbs the errors in the survey's income, its spending and the tax estimate. Treat it as the least precise branch.
  • Shelter for owners counts mortgage interest, property tax, insurance and upkeep, not the purchase price of the home. Deflating it by the rent-based housing parity is the standard approximation.
  • Real income here is the sum of the deflated branches, so it differs slightly from nominal income divided by the all-items parity.
